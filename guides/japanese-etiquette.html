Japanese etiquette: 15 rules every visitor should know

Shoes off or on? Tip or not? How do you pray at a shrine, and why is everyone so quiet on the train? The 15 rules that matter most, explained simply, so you can relax and enjoy Japan.

By Gabriel P. · Updated 19 November 2026 · 8 min read
IN THIS GUIDEShort answerShoes, homes and ryokan (1–2)Money and greetings (3–4)At the table (5–6)Trains and streets (7–10)Temples and onsen (11–12)People and space (13–15)The cheat sheet

The short answer

Japanese people don't expect visitors to know every rule, and they are very forgiving. But five things really matter: take your shoes off where there is a step up, don't tip, no phone calls on trains, carry your rubbish and wash before getting into an onsen. The best trick of all? Watch what the people around you do, and do the same.

Shoes, homes and ryokan

1. Take your shoes off where there is a step up

At homes, ryokan, many temples, some restaurants and even fitting rooms, there is an entrance area (genkan) one step lower than the floor. Shoes stay there, toes pointing to the door; you step up in socks or the slippers provided. Never walk on tatami mats in slippers: socks or bare feet only. Wear socks without holes and shoes that are easy to slip on and off.

2. Use the toilet slippers, and only there

Many homes and ryokan have special slippers inside the toilet. Change into them at the door and change back when you leave: walking out into the corridor or the dining room in toilet slippers is the classic tourist mistake (and everyone notices).

Money and greetings

3. Don't tip

Tipping is not part of the culture and can cause embarrassment: staff may run after you to return the money. Good service is included. In restaurants and shops put your cash or card on the small tray by the till instead of handing it over.

4. A small bow goes a long way

Nobody expects a perfect bow from a visitor, but a slight nod of the head when you say thank you (arigatō gozaimasu), greet someone or apologise is always appreciated. Handshakes are fine with business people; hugs and back-slaps are not.

At the table

5. Chopsticks: two things you must never do

Never stick chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice and never pass food from chopsticks to chopsticks: both recall funeral rites. Also avoid pointing with them, licking them or rubbing disposable ones together. When you pause, lay them on the rest or across your bowl.

6. Slurp your noodles, but don't blow your nose

Slurping ramen or soba is normal and shows you're enjoying it. Blowing your nose at the table is not: step outside or to the toilet. Use the oshibori (wet towel) for your hands only, not your face. Say itadakimasu before eating and gochisōsama deshita when you leave: more in our restaurant phrases guide.

Trains and streets

7. Be quiet on trains and buses

No phone calls on public transport: set your phone to silent ("manner mode"). Talk quietly, keep your backpack on your front or on the rack and leave priority seats to people who need them. Eating is fine on the shinkansen and long-distance trains, but not on local commuter trains.

8. Queue, and stand on the right side of the escalator

People line up in neat rows on the platform markings. On escalators, the unwritten rule is to stand on the left in Tokyo and on the right in Osaka, although stations now ask everyone to stand on both sides and not walk: follow what locals around you do.

9. Don't eat while walking

In many places, eating and drinking while walking down the street is frowned upon, and some busy streets near temples (for example in Kamakura and around Asakusa) ask visitors not to. Eat next to the stall or vending machine where you bought it.

10. Carry your rubbish with you

Japan is spotless but has very few public bins. Keep a small bag for your trash and throw it away at your hotel, at a convenience store bin (when you buy something there) or at stations. Separate burnable, plastic and bottles/cans where bins are marked.

Temples and onsen

11. At shrines: purify, bow, clap

At the chōzuya water basin rinse your left hand, then your right, then pour a little water into your left hand to rinse your mouth (don't drink from the ladle). At a Shinto shrine the prayer is: bow twice, clap twice, pray, bow once. At a Buddhist temple you don't clap: just put your hands together quietly. Walk on the sides of the path, not the centre.

12. Onsen: wash first, no swimsuit, no towel in the water

In hot springs and public baths you bathe naked, separated by gender. Wash and rinse thoroughly at the shower stools before getting in; keep the small towel out of the water (people put it on their head). Tie up long hair. Many baths still refuse tattoos: check in advance or look for "tattoo friendly" baths or private baths (kashikiri).

People and space

13. Ask before photographing people

Never photograph geisha or maiko as if they were an attraction, and don't follow them. In Kyoto's Gion, signs warn of fines of up to ¥10,000 for entering or taking photos on private lanes. Respect "no photo" signs in temples, shops and restaurants too.

14. Mind your volume and your space

Japanese public spaces are calm. Avoid loud groups on the street at night, don't block narrow pavements with big suitcases (use luggage forwarding, takkyūbin) and don't sit on temple steps or walls where it's not allowed.

15. Wear a mask if you have a cold

Wearing a mask when you're coughing or sneezing is seen as basic consideration for others. Nobody minds if you wear one; many people will mind if you cough openly on a crowded train.

FAQ

Quick answers.

Should I tip in Japan?

No. Tipping is not customary in Japan and staff may politely refuse it. Good service is included in the price; just say thank you (arigatō gozaimasu).

Do I have to take my shoes off in Japan?

Yes, wherever there is a raised entrance step: homes, ryokan, many temples, some restaurants and fitting rooms. Never walk on tatami in slippers.

Is it rude to eat while walking in Japan?

It is generally frowned upon and some busy streets ask visitors not to. Eat next to the stall or vending machine where you bought the food.

Can I go to an onsen with tattoos?

Many onsen and public baths still refuse tattoos. Look for tattoo-friendly baths, cover small tattoos with a sticker where allowed, or book a private bath (kashikiri).

Which side of the escalator do you stand on in Japan?

Traditionally on the left in Tokyo and on the right in Osaka. Many stations now ask people to stand still on both sides, so follow what locals do.
