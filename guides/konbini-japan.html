Konbini in Japan: what to buy and what they can do for you

7‑Eleven, FamilyMart and Lawson are open 24 hours and do far more than snacks: cash, hot meals, tickets, printing, sending luggage. Here's how to use them like a local.

By Gabriel P. · Updated 26 November 2026 · 7 min read

The short answer

A konbini (コンビニ, convenience store) is your best friend in Japan. There's one on almost every corner, most are open 24 hours, and you can get a good cheap meal, withdraw cash with a foreign card, top up your Suica, print documents and even ship your suitcase. Many tourists end up visiting one every day.

You'll also see smaller chains such as Ministop (with eat-in corners) and, in Hokkaido, Seicomart — locals love its hot food.

What to eat

  • Onigiri (rice balls): tuna-mayo, salmon, pickled plum. Open the wrapper in order 1 → 2 → 3 (numbers on the plastic) so the seaweed stays crispy.
  • Bento boxes: full meals with rice, meat or fish — staff heat them for you.
  • Egg sandwich (tamago sando): soft bread, creamy egg, a cult favourite.
  • Fried chicken by the counter: Famichiki at FamilyMart, Karaage‑kun at Lawson.
  • Oden in winter: broth with egg, daikon and fish cakes, picked from the pot by the till.
  • Sweets and drinks: seasonal desserts, cold green tea, hot canned coffee from the heated shelf in winter.
On a budget? An onigiri, an egg sandwich and a drink make a real breakfast for well under ¥600. See what a full day costs in How much does a trip to Japan cost.

Paying — and the 3 questions at the till

Cards, IC cards like Suica and PASMO and cash are all accepted. Put cash in the small tray on the counter rather than in the cashier's hand. Staff will often ask you three quick questions:

温めますか？
Atatamemasu ka?
Shall I heat it up? — answer “Hai” (yes) or “Daijōbu desu” (no thanks)
袋はご利用ですか？
Fukuro wa go-riyō desu ka?
Do you need a bag? Plastic bags cost a few yen
お箸はお付けしますか？
Ohashi wa otsuke shimasu ka?
Would you like chopsticks? (or a spoon: “supūn”)
袋はいりません
Fukuro wa irimasen
I don't need a bag

Cash, tickets, printing and luggage

  • ATMs: 7‑Eleven's Seven Bank ATMs accept most foreign Visa, Mastercard, Amex, JCB and UnionPay cards, with menus in 12 languages. Your bank may add its own fee.
  • Top up Suica/PASMO at the till or at the ATM.
  • Tax-free: some larger 7‑Eleven stores offer tax-free shopping with your passport — look for the sign.
  • Multicopy machines: print boarding passes, documents and photos.
  • Tickets: concerts, museums and theme parks can often be paid or collected at the in-store machine.
  • Luggage forwarding (takkyubin): send your suitcase to the next hotel or the airport and travel light on the Shinkansen.
  • Toilets: many konbini have one; ask politely and buy something as a thank you.

Konbini etiquette

  • Don't eat while walking: eat in the store's eat-in area if there is one, or just outside.
  • Rubbish: public bins are rare in Japan. Use the bins near the konbini entrance for what you bought there, and sort it (cans, bottles, burnables).
  • Queue in the line marked on the floor, not at a random till.
  • Speak quietly and keep phone calls short inside. More tips in Japanese etiquette for tourists.

Prices and services are indicative and vary by store — check in-store signs.

FAQ

Quick answers.

What is a konbini in Japan?

A konbini is a Japanese convenience store such as 7‑Eleven, FamilyMart or Lawson. Most are open 24 hours and sell meals, drinks and everyday items, and offer services like ATMs and printing.

Can I withdraw cash at a konbini with a foreign card?

Yes. Seven Bank ATMs in 7‑Eleven accept most foreign cards and have English menus; Lawson and FamilyMart ATMs work with many cards too. Your bank may charge a fee.

Can I pay by card at Japanese convenience stores?

Yes, major cards and IC cards like Suica and PASMO are widely accepted, but carry some cash as acceptance is not universal.

How much does a konbini meal cost?

An onigiri and a drink cost about ¥300–500; a bento box about ¥500–700.
