Milan in 2 days: the perfect itinerary

The Duomo rooftop, Leonardo's Last Supper, the Brera district and aperitivo on the canals: a relaxed two-day plan for Milan, with what to book in advance, where to sleep and what to eat.

The short answer

Two days are enough to see the best of Milan without rushing. Day 1: the Duomo and its rooftop terraces, the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II, La Scala and the artists' district of Brera. Day 2: Leonardo's Last Supper, the Sforza Castle and Sempione Park, then aperitivo on the Navigli canals. The one thing you must book well in advance is the Last Supper.

Day 1: Duomo, Galleria and Brera

Morning – the Duomo. Start early in Piazza del Duomo, when the square is still quiet. The cathedral took almost six centuries to finish, and the best part is walking on the roof among the marble spires, with the golden Madonnina above you and, on clear days, the Alps on the horizon. Buy a combined ticket that includes the terraces (lift or stairs) and the cathedral; the lift version costs more but saves your legs. Shoulders and knees must be covered inside.

Late morning – the Galleria and La Scala. Next to the Duomo, the glass-roofed Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II is one of the oldest shopping arcades in the world. Locals spin on their heel on the bull mosaic for good luck. Walk through it to Piazza della Scala: the opera house looks plain from outside, but the small theatre museum lets you peek into the auditorium when there is no rehearsal.

Afternoon – Brera. Ten minutes on foot to the north, Brera is Milan's prettiest neighbourhood: cobbled streets, galleries, small shops and cafés. The Pinacoteca di Brera holds Italian masterpieces such as Mantegna's Dead Christ and Caravaggio's Supper at Emmaus; next door the old Botanical Garden is a free, quiet break.

Evening. Dinner in Brera or around Corso Garibaldi. If you like modern architecture, walk on to Piazza Gae Aulenti and the Bosco Verticale, the two tower blocks covered in trees, beautifully lit at night.

Day 2: Last Supper, Castle and Navigli

Morning – the Last Supper. Leonardo da Vinci's Last Supper is painted on the wall of the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie. To protect it, small groups enter at fixed times for about 15 minutes. It is short but unforgettable. Arrive 20 minutes before your slot to collect the ticket. Visit the church itself afterwards: it is free.

Late morning – Sforza Castle. Walk 15 minutes to the Castello Sforzesco, the red-brick fortress of the Dukes of Milan. The courtyards are free; the museums inside include Michelangelo's last sculpture, the unfinished Rondanini Pietà. Behind the castle, Parco Sempione leads to the Arco della Pace, perfect for a picnic or a rest.

Afternoon – Sant'Ambrogio and shopping. The Romanesque Basilica of Sant'Ambrogio is one of the oldest churches in the city and almost always quiet. If you prefer shopping, the Quadrilatero della Moda (Via Montenapoleone and Via della Spiga) is the luxury fashion district; Corso Buenos Aires and Via Torino are cheaper.

Evening – aperitivo on the Navigli. The Navigli are the old canals in the south of the city. From about 6 pm the bars along Naviglio Grande and Naviglio Pavese fill up for aperitivo: you order a Spritz or a Negroni Sbagliato (invented in Milan) and get snacks, sometimes a whole buffet. On the last Sunday of the month the Naviglio Grande hosts a big antiques market.

Beware of fake sellers. Many websites resell Last Supper tickets at high prices. Buy from the official site or from a well-known tour operator, and check the exact date and time on your ticket.

Arriving and getting around

  • From Malpensa: the Malpensa Express train reaches Cadorna or Centrale in about 40–55 minutes. Buses to Centrale are a bit cheaper but slower in traffic.
  • From Linate: the M4 metro line takes you to the centre (San Babila) in about 15 minutes for the price of a normal ticket.
  • In the city: metro, trams and buses use the same ticket. You can simply tap a contactless card or phone at the metro gates: the system charges the right fare and caps it at the daily price.
  • On foot: Duomo, Galleria, La Scala, Brera and the Castle are all within a 20-minute walk of each other.
  • Driving: don't. The centre is a paid congestion zone (Area C) watched by cameras: read our ZTL guide before renting a car.

Where to stay in Milan

  • Duomo / Centro Storico: everything on foot, but the most expensive.
  • Brera: charming, lively in the evening, close to everything.
  • Centrale: practical for trains and the Malpensa Express, many good-value hotels; quieter streets are better at night.
  • Porta Romana / Navigli: local atmosphere, great for evenings out, well connected by metro.

The city adds a tourist tax per person per night, paid at the hotel. During Design Week (April) and the fashion weeks prices rise sharply.

What to eat in Milan

  • Risotto alla milanese, yellow with saffron, often served with ossobuco (braised veal shank).
  • Cotoletta alla milanese: a thick breaded veal chop fried in butter.
  • Panzerotti from the famous bakery near the Duomo: queue moves fast.
  • Panettone: Milan's Christmas cake, at its best in November and December.
  • Aperitivo: born here, and still the best-value "dinner" in town.

Prices in Milan are among the highest in Italy: see our Italy trip cost guide and, for good manners at the table, eating out in Italy.

With a third day

  • Lake Como: trains to Varenna or Como in about 1 hour, then ferries between the villages.
  • Bergamo: about 50 minutes by train, with a beautiful walled upper town reached by funicular.
  • Venice or Turin: both under 2h30 by high-speed train if you are moving on.

FAQ

Quick answers.

Are 2 days enough for Milan?

Yes. Two days are enough for the Duomo and its terraces, the Galleria, La Scala, Brera, the Last Supper, the Sforza Castle and an evening on the Navigli. Add a third day for Lake Como or Bergamo.

How far in advance should I book the Last Supper?

As early as possible: official tickets usually open about 2–3 months ahead and sell out quickly. If they are gone, look for guided tours that include entry.

Is it worth going up to the Duomo terraces?

Yes, it is the highlight of the Duomo: you walk among the marble spires with views over the city and, on clear days, the Alps.

How do I get from Malpensa to Milan?

The Malpensa Express train reaches Cadorna or Centrale in about 40–55 minutes. Buses to Centrale are a little cheaper but slower.

Can I drive in the centre of Milan?

It is better not to: the centre is Area C, a paid congestion zone watched by cameras. Use the metro, trams and your feet.
