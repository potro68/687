How to ask for an English menu in Japan

One short, polite sentence works in most restaurants. Here is the phrase, how to say it, what you may hear back, and what to do when there is no English menu.

Updated 25 October 2026 · 5 min read · By the 687 team

The phrase to use

Catch a staff member’s attention, then ask:

英語のメニューはありますか？
Eigo no menyū wa arimasu ka?
“Do you have an English menu?”

To call staff politely, start with すみません (sumimasen, “excuse me”). In many restaurants it is normal to say it out loud from your table, or to press the call button if there is one.

If you are not comfortable speaking, simply show the phrase on your phone screen. Staff are used to it and it avoids any pronunciation worries.

How to pronounce it

Say it slowly and evenly. Japanese has no strong stress, so every syllable gets about the same weight:

  • Ei-go — “AY-go” (English)
  • no — like “no”
  • me-nyū — “MEH-nyoo” (menu)
  • wa — “wah”
  • a-ri-ma-su ka — “ah-ree-MAS-kah”; the final u in masu is almost silent

Together: “AY-go no MEH-nyoo wah ah-ree-MAS-kah?” with a light rising tone at the end, like a question.

Answers you may hear

はい、あります
Hai, arimasu
Yes, we have one.
すみません、ありません
Sumimasen, arimasen
Sorry, we don’t.
少々お待ちください
Shōshō omachi kudasai
One moment, please.
こちらです
Kochira desu
Here you are / this way.

No English menu? Use these phrases

Many small, family-run places only have a Japanese menu. That is rarely a problem — these phrases cover most situations:

写真付きのメニューはありますか？
Shashin-tsuki no menyū wa arimasu ka?
Do you have a menu with pictures?
これをください
Kore o kudasai
This one, please (while pointing).
おすすめは何ですか？
Osusume wa nan desu ka?
What do you recommend?
お会計お願いします
O-kaikei onegaishimasu
The bill, please.

You can also point at the plastic food models in the window, or at a dish another table is eating, and say kore o kudasai. When you leave, ごちそうさまでした (gochisōsama deshita, “thank you for the meal”) is always appreciated.

How ordering often works in Japan

Ticket machines (食券機, shokkenki)

Common in ramen, soba and curry shops. You choose and pay at the machine near the entrance, then hand the ticket to staff. Many machines have photos, and some have an English button.

Tablet and QR-code ordering

Chains, izakaya and conveyor-belt sushi often use tablets or a QR code on the table. These systems frequently offer an English option — look for a language button or a flag icon.

Izakaya table charge

In many izakaya you receive a small starter you did not order (otōshi). It is a normal table charge, not a mistake.

Tipping

Tipping is not part of restaurant culture in Japan. Paying the bill is enough, and good service is simply expected.

Where English menus are more likely

  • Busy tourist areas such as central Tokyo, Kyoto and Osaka.
  • Restaurant chains and places inside large stations and department stores.
  • Places with an English name on the sign or an English website.
  • Restaurants that use tablet or QR-code ordering.

None of these is a guarantee, so it is still worth having the phrase ready.

FAQ

Quick answers.

Is it rude to ask for an English menu in Japan?

No. It is a normal, polite request, especially in areas popular with visitors. Start with “sumimasen” and staff will be happy to help.

What does “Eigo no menyū wa arimasu ka” mean?

It means “Do you have an English menu?”. Eigo means English, menyū is menu, and “arimasu ka” turns it into a polite question.

What if the restaurant has no English menu?

Ask for a picture menu (“shashin-tsuki no menyū wa arimasu ka?”), point at dishes and say “kore o kudasai”, or ask for a recommendation with “osusume wa nan desu ka?”.

Do I need to speak Japanese to eat out in Japan?

No. Showing a phrase on your phone, pointing at photos or food models and using a translation app is enough in almost every restaurant.
